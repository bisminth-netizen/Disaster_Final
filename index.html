Natural Disaster – Definition (UNDRR 2016)

A serious disruption of functioning of a community or society, causing widespread human, material, economic, or environmental losses that exceed the ability of the affected community to cope using its own resources.

3 Elements: (1) Disruption of functioning, (2) Impact (losses), (3) Overwhelmed capacity

IPCC AR6 Definition (2022)

Emphasizes the interaction of hazard × vulnerability — a disaster results from the combination of a hazardous event and conditions of vulnerability that together result in significant disruptions.

🧠 Key Difference: UNDRR = capacity-centric (overwhelmed capacity). IPCC = process-centric (hazard × vulnerability interaction).

🌍Lecture 3: Spatial Referencing & Satellite-Based Positioning
🔥 HOT
Two Reference Surfaces for Earth Approximation

🌊 GEOID

  • Gravity-based equipotential surface
  • Closely approximated by mean sea level
  • Undulating, irregular shape
  • Used for: Orthometric Height (H)
  • "What water flows along"
  • Measured by: tide gauges (mareographs)

📐 ELLIPSOID

  • Mathematical/geometric surface
  • Smooth, regular shape
  • Defined by semi-major (a) and semi-minor (b) axes
  • Used for: Ellipsoidal Height (h)
  • Used for horizontal coordinates
  • GPS uses ellipsoid (WGS84)
Geoid Separation (N) = h − H

N can be positive or negative depending on location. GPS gives h; for practical engineering use, convert to H using N.

🧠 Memory: Geoid = Gravity, Ground, Geography (physical). Ellipsoid = Equation, Exact math. H = Height you feel walking uphill (physical). h = hidden from reality (mathematical).

Datum Transformation Workflow
Map Coordinates (A) → Geographic (lat/lon) → Datum Transform → Geographic (lat/lon) → Map Coordinates (B)

You cannot transform directly between two projected coordinate systems — you must go through geographic coordinates first, apply datum transformation, then re-project.

Local vs Global Datums
  • Local Horizontal Datum: Created by triangulation/trilateration from a reference point; fits local region well but not globally consistent
  • Global Datum (WGS84): Geocentric; consistent worldwide; used by GPS/GNSS; may not match local heights
🛰️Satellite-Based Positioning (GNSS/GPS)
🔥 HOT
Why 4 Satellites Are Needed for 3D Positioning

GPS positioning uses pseudoranges — distance measurements that contain a receiver clock bias error.

4 unknowns: X, Y, Z (position) + Δt (clock bias) → need 4 equations = 4 satellites
SatellitesSolves For
3 satellites2D position (X, Y) only — clock error not solved
4 satellitesFull 3D position (X, Y, Z) + clock bias correction ✓
5+ satellitesRedundancy, improved accuracy, integrity monitoring
🧠 Memory: "3 for space, 1 for time" → 3 satellites define your 3D location, the 4th satellite corrects your clock. 4 unknowns need 4 equations — it's basic algebra!
🎯 Exam Trick: Pseudorange = measured distance, but because your receiver clock is imperfect, it's "pseudo" (not exact). The 4th satellite eliminates this unknown. Always mention "clock bias" in your answer.

Q1 Define "natural disaster" according to UNDRR (2016) and explain its three key elements. ▼
Lecture 1🔥 Very Likely
MODEL ANSWER
According to UNDRR (2016), a natural disaster is "a serious disruption of the functioning of a community or society at any scale, caused by hazardous events interacting with conditions of exposure, vulnerability, and capacity, leading to one or more of the following: human, material, economic, and environmental losses and impacts." It has three key elements: First, disruption of functioning — normal activities of society are interrupted. Second, impact — widespread human, economic, or environmental losses occur. Third, overwhelmed capacity — the community's own resources are insufficient to cope with the event. All three elements must be present for an event to qualify as a disaster.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 DIO: Disruption + Impact + Overwhelmed capacity = Disaster. No DIO = no disaster.
Q2 How does the IPCC AR6 (2022) definition of disaster differ from UNDRR's definition? ▼
Lecture 1
MODEL ANSWER
While both definitions describe disasters as events causing significant disruption and losses, they differ in their emphasis. UNDRR (2016) is capacity-centric — it defines a disaster through the lens of a community's overwhelmed capacity to cope. IPCC AR6 (2022) is process-centric — it emphasizes the interaction between hazard and vulnerability as the mechanism that produces disasters. The IPCC definition highlights that disasters are not just about the physical hazard itself, but about the dynamic interplay between hazardous events and the socioeconomic conditions of vulnerability. This makes the IPCC framing more useful for climate-related disasters where exposure and vulnerability are increasingly shaped by global change.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 UNDRR = "Can't cope" (capacity). IPCC = "H×V interaction" (process). Two lenses, same disaster.
Q3 Describe the four phases of the Disaster Management Cycle and explain how Earth Observation supports each phase. ▼
Lecture 1🔥 Likely
MODEL ANSWER
The Disaster Management Cycle is a continuous loop of four phases. Preparedness involves planning, training, and establishing early warning systems before disasters occur; EO supports this through hazard mapping, vulnerability assessment, and monitoring early warning indicators. Response covers immediate actions during or just after a disaster, such as search and rescue; EO provides rapid damage assessments, flood extent mapping, and situational awareness. Recovery focuses on rebuilding communities and restoring services; EO enables change detection to monitor reconstruction progress and assess remaining needs. Mitigation aims to reduce future risk through long-term measures like land-use planning; EO contributes through multi-temporal hazard mapping and exposure analysis. Each phase feeds into the next, making the cycle iterative rather than linear.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "Pretty Roses Rarely Manage" → Preparedness, Response, Recovery, Mitigation
Q4 List and briefly explain the five priorities of the Hyogo Framework for Action (HFA, 2005–2015). ▼
Lecture 1🔥 Likely
MODEL ANSWER
The Hyogo Framework for Action (2005–2015) established five priorities for disaster risk reduction. Priority 1 is to ensure that DRR becomes a national and local priority with a strong institutional basis, meaning governments must integrate risk reduction into policy. Priority 2 is to identify, assess, and monitor disaster risks and enhance early warning, so communities have timely information to act on threats. Priority 3 is to use knowledge, innovation, and education to build a culture of safety and resilience at all levels. Priority 4 is to reduce the underlying risk factors, addressing poverty, environmental degradation, and poor urban development. Priority 5 is to strengthen disaster preparedness for effective response at all levels, ensuring systems are in place before disaster strikes.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "National Identification Keeps Reducing Strengthening" → Institutional priority, Identify risks, Knowledge/education, Reduce risk factors, Strengthen preparedness
Q5 What are the four priorities of the Sendai Framework (SFDRR, 2015–2030), and how do they differ from HFA? ▼
Lecture 1🔥 Very Likely
MODEL ANSWER
The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030) has four priorities. Priority 1 is Understanding disaster risk — comprehending hazards, exposure, vulnerability, and capacity. Priority 2 is Strengthening disaster risk governance — improved coordination across sectors and levels. Priority 3 is Investing in DRR for resilience — directing resources toward prevention. Priority 4 is Enhancing disaster preparedness for effective response and to "Build Back Better" — ensuring recovery leads to improved resilience. Compared to HFA, SFDRR is more proactive: it aims to prevent the creation of new risk rather than just manage existing risk. It also broadens stakeholder participation beyond government to include the private sector, science community, and civil society. Crucially, SFDRR introduced seven global targets with measurable indicators for monitoring progress, which HFA lacked.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "UGIB" → Understand, Governance, Invest, Build Back Better. SFDRR = "Everyone + Proactive + Measure it."
Q6 Explain the disaster risk formula and describe what each component means. How can risk be reduced? ▼
Lecture 1🔥 Very Likely
MODEL ANSWER
Disaster risk is expressed as: Risk (R) = Hazard (H) × Exposure (Ex) × Vulnerability (V). Hazard is a potentially damaging physical phenomenon — such as an earthquake, flood, or cyclone — that has the potential to cause harm. Exposure refers to people, assets, infrastructure, or systems located in hazard-prone zones. Vulnerability describes the susceptibility of exposed elements to damage, rooted in social, economic, physical, or environmental conditions. Risk can be reduced by acting on any component: reducing hazard impact (through early warning or engineered barriers), reducing exposure (through land-use zoning or relocation), or reducing vulnerability (through stronger buildings, social safety nets, and capacity building). The formula reveals that a hazard alone does not create a disaster — it requires exposed and vulnerable populations to become a risk, and ultimately a disaster if capacity is overwhelmed.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "HEV like a car" → Hazard = road danger, Exposure = car on the road, Vulnerability = worn brakes. Fix any one = safer drive.
Q7 Explain the PAR (Pressure and Release) model of disaster risk. What does "releasing pressure" mean in practice? ▼
Lecture 1🔥 Very Likely
MODEL ANSWER
The PAR (Pressure and Release) model explains how disasters result from the intersection of social vulnerabilities and physical hazards. On one side, vulnerability is "built up" through three levels of social conditions: Root Causes (deep structural factors such as poverty, political marginalization, and lack of resources), Dynamic Pressures (processes that translate root causes into vulnerable conditions, such as rapid urbanization, population growth, and lack of local institutions), and Unsafe Conditions (specific physical or social contexts like fragile housing, hazardous locations, and weak livelihoods). On the other side, physical hazards press against these unsafe conditions. A disaster occurs when the two sides meet. "Releasing pressure" means addressing root causes — eliminating poverty, empowering communities, improving governance — so that the chain of vulnerability is broken before a hazard event strikes.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 Pressure Cooker: Root causes = heat source, Dynamic pressures = building steam, Unsafe conditions = weak valve, Hazard = the moment it explodes. Release = turn off the heat (fix root causes).
Q8 What is a cascading hazard? Provide a real-world example and explain why it is challenging for disaster management. ▼
Lecture 1
MODEL ANSWER
A cascading hazard occurs when a primary hazard event triggers one or more secondary hazard events in a chain reaction. A classic example is the 2011 Tōhoku event: the earthquake (primary hazard) triggered a massive tsunami (secondary hazard), which then caused the Fukushima nuclear plant failure (tertiary hazard). Cascading hazards are particularly challenging for disaster management for several reasons. First, response plans designed for one hazard type may be inadequate or counterproductive for subsequent hazards. Second, the cumulative impact is far greater than any single event, overwhelming emergency services. Third, the timing and nature of secondary hazards may be difficult to predict. Effective management requires multi-hazard early warning systems, cross-sectoral coordination, and scenario planning that anticipates cascade chains rather than treating each hazard in isolation.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 Domino Effect: Cascading = dominos. One falls → knocks the next → chain reaction. Earthquake → Tsunami → Nuclear meltdown = one cascade.
Q9 Name and briefly describe the six types of hazard interactions in a multi-disaster context. ▼
Lecture 1🔥 Likely
MODEL ANSWER
In a multi-disaster context, hazards can interact in six distinct ways. (1) Parallel hazards occur simultaneously but independently in different locations. (2) Cascading hazards involve a chain reaction where one hazard triggers subsequent hazards. (3) Disposition alteration occurs when a first hazard changes the environment, increasing susceptibility to a second hazard — for example, drought weakening soil cohesion leading to landslides after rain. (4) Additional hazard potential arises when the first hazard creates conditions for new hazards, such as an earthquake creating unstable slopes. (5) Coincident triggering occurs when two simultaneous hazards combine to produce a third — for example, heavy rain and saturated soils together triggering landslides. (6) Cyclic triggering describes regular seasonal patterns of hazard occurrence, such as annual monsoon flooding.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "PC-DA-CC" → Parallel, Cascading, Disposition, Additional, Coincident, Cyclic. Think of "PC Desktop Application Cycling."
Q10 Define remote sensing and explain the role of electromagnetic radiation as an information carrier in this field. ▼
Lecture 1🔥 Very Likely
MODEL ANSWER
Remote sensing is defined as "the art, science, and technology of observing an object, scene, or phenomenon by instrument-based techniques from a distance, without physical contact." The word "remote" means not in direct contact, while "sensing" means detecting information about the target. Electromagnetic radiation (EMR) serves as the primary information carrier in remote sensing. Different features of the Earth's surface and atmosphere interact with EMR in characteristic ways — absorbing, reflecting, transmitting, or emitting energy at different wavelengths. Sensors on satellites or aircraft measure this EMR to infer physical properties such as vegetation cover, soil moisture, urban extent, or temperature. Passive sensors detect naturally reflected or emitted EMR (e.g., sunlight reflected from the ground), while active sensors emit their own EMR and measure the return signal, enabling observations regardless of natural light conditions.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "See from far, measure light" → Remote = far, Sensing = measuring EMR interaction. Passive = uses sun's light. Active = has its own flashlight (SAR, LiDAR).
Q11 Compare passive and active remote sensing sensors. Why are active sensors (SAR) particularly valuable in disaster response? ▼
Lecture 1
MODEL ANSWER
Passive sensors detect electromagnetic radiation that originates from external sources, primarily the sun. Optical sensors (such as Sentinel-2 and Landsat) are passive — they record sunlight reflected from Earth's surface. They produce visually interpretable images but are limited by cloud cover, smoke, and darkness, which block their view. Active sensors, in contrast, generate and transmit their own electromagnetic pulses and measure the energy returning from the target. Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), for example Sentinel-1, emits microwave energy that penetrates clouds, smoke, and darkness, providing reliable imagery under any weather conditions. In disaster response, this is critical because disasters often generate thick cloud cover, smoke from fires, or occur at night. SAR can also detect subtle surface deformation, detect flooded areas under vegetation canopy, and monitor structural changes, making it indispensable for rapid damage assessment when optical data is unavailable.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 Torch analogy: Passive = camera using sunlight (useless at night). Active = camera with its own torch (works anytime, anywhere). SAR torch = microwave energy that goes through clouds.
Q12 What key findings from GAR 2022 reveal about the global pattern and economic impact of disasters? ▼
Lecture 1🔥 Likely
MODEL ANSWER
The Global Assessment Report on Disaster Risk Reduction (GAR) 2022 reveals several critical patterns. Between 2000 and 2019, there were 7,348 recorded natural disasters, resulting in approximately 1.23 million deaths and affecting 4.2 billion people (many counted multiple times). Economic losses totalled USD 2.97 trillion. Between 2015 and 2021, the annual average cost of disasters reached USD 330 billion. The geographic pattern of mortality is starkly unequal — 91% of all disaster-related deaths occurred in developing countries, highlighting the disproportionate burden borne by nations with lower adaptive capacity. Economically, developed nations suffer greater absolute financial losses, but developing nations suffer more in proportion to their GDP. The report also demonstrates the cost-effectiveness of prevention: every USD 1 invested in disaster risk reduction saves USD 5 to 10 in future disaster losses.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "7-1-4-3 formula" → 7,348 disasters, 1.23M deaths, 4.2B affected, $2.97 trillion losses. Plus: 91% deaths in developing nations, $1 DRR = $5–10 savings.
Q13 Summarize the key commitments of the Paris Agreement (COP21, 2015) and explain its relevance to disaster risk. ▼
Lecture 1
MODEL ANSWER
The Paris Agreement, adopted at COP21 in December 2015, is an international treaty aimed at limiting human-induced climate change. Its central commitment is to hold global temperature rise to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, while pursuing efforts to limit warming to 1.5°C — a threshold critical for avoiding the most severe climate impacts. Countries participate by submitting Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), which are self-determined climate action plans, to be revised and strengthened every five years. Developed nations committed to providing at least USD 100 billion per year in climate finance to support developing nations. The Paris Agreement is directly relevant to disaster risk because climate change intensifies the frequency and severity of extreme weather events — including storms, floods, droughts, and heatwaves — which increases disaster risk globally. Without climate mitigation, the underlying hazard component of the risk equation will continue to grow.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "2-1.5-5-100" → <2°C ceiling, aim for 1.5°C, NDC every 5 years, $100B/year finance. Climate action = disaster prevention.
Q14 What is systems thinking, and why is it important in the context of Disaster Risk Management? ▼
Lecture 1
MODEL ANSWER
Systems thinking is a holistic, integrative, and cross-disciplinary approach to analysis that focuses on understanding how a system's components interrelate and how systems work over time and within larger systems. It contrasts with reductionism, which breaks a problem into isolated parts. In the context of DRM, systems thinking recognizes that disasters are not merely physical events but products of complex interactions between social, economic, environmental, and governance systems. For example, flooding in an urban area involves not just the hydrology (physical system) but also urban planning decisions (governance), socioeconomic inequality (social system), and land degradation (environmental system). Systems thinking helps DRM practitioners identify feedback loops, unintended consequences, and leverage points — places where small changes produce large improvements in resilience. It supports more integrated, sustainable disaster risk reduction strategies that address root causes rather than symptoms.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 Systems = Whole picture. Reductionism = puzzle pieces. DRM needs the whole puzzle, not just one piece.
Q15 Define vulnerability in the context of disaster risk and explain the factors that contribute to it. ▼
Lecture 1
MODEL ANSWER
Vulnerability is defined as the conditions determined by physical, social, economic, and environmental factors or processes that increase the susceptibility of an individual, a community, assets, or systems to the impacts of hazards. It represents the degree to which a community or system is unable to resist, absorb, cope with, or recover from a hazardous event. Physical vulnerability includes poorly constructed buildings or infrastructure in hazard-prone locations. Social vulnerability encompasses poverty, marginalization, lack of access to information, and weak social networks. Economic vulnerability involves dependence on hazard-exposed livelihoods, limited insurance, and lack of savings. Environmental vulnerability includes ecosystem degradation that removes natural buffers such as wetlands or mangroves that reduce flood impact. Critically, vulnerability is not fixed — it can be reduced through targeted DRR measures such as building codes, social protection, education, and ecosystem restoration.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 PSEE: Physical + Social + Economic + Environmental = vulnerability. Think "People Struggle Especially Exposed."
Q16 Define the Geoid and explain why it is used as a reference surface for measuring heights. ▼
Lecture 3🔥 Very Likely
MODEL ANSWER
The Geoid is an equipotential surface of Earth's gravity field that closely corresponds to the mean sea level of the undisturbed ocean and its imaginary continuation under the continents. It is an undulating, irregular surface — not a perfect sphere or ellipsoid — because Earth's internal mass distribution is uneven, causing variations in gravitational attraction. The Geoid is used as the reference surface for measuring orthometric heights (H), also called physical heights or heights above sea level. This is because the Geoid corresponds to the natural flow of water: water flows from high to low potential, which corresponds to downhill on the Geoid. Any survey of heights that must be physically meaningful — such as for drainage design, flood modelling, or infrastructure — must reference the Geoid. The mean sea level at coastal locations is measured using tide gauges (mareographs), which collectively define the local Geoid realization.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "Geoid = where water goes" → Gravity controls water flow; Geoid is the surface water follows. If your height is measured from the Geoid, water flows downhill — makes physical sense. Orthometric Height H = "Height above sea level."
Q17 What is a reference ellipsoid? How is it defined mathematically, and what is it used for? ▼
Lecture 3🔥 Likely
MODEL ANSWER
A reference ellipsoid is a mathematically defined, smooth, oblate spheroid that serves as a simplified geometric approximation of Earth's shape. Unlike the Geoid, which is physically motivated by gravity, the ellipsoid is purely geometric. It is defined by two parameters: the semi-major axis (a), which is the equatorial radius, and the semi-minor axis (b), the polar radius. The degree of flattening is described by the flattening parameter f = (a − b) / a. The ellipsoid is used as the reference surface for horizontal geodetic coordinates — latitude (φ) and longitude (λ) are measured on the ellipsoid. GPS systems use the WGS84 ellipsoid (a = 6,378,137 m; f = 1/298.257), while different countries have historically used locally fitted ellipsoids that best match their territory. Because the ellipsoid is smooth and mathematically tractable, it is ideal for global navigation and coordinate calculations.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "a=Equator, b=Pole, f=(a-b)/a" → Flat at poles (b < a), so f is positive (Earth is squished). WGS84: a=6,378,137; f=1/298 ≈ tiny.
Q18 Explain the difference between orthometric height (H) and ellipsoidal height (h), and define the geoid separation (N). ▼
Lecture 3🔥 Very Likely
MODEL ANSWER
Orthometric height (H) is the height of a point measured vertically above the Geoid — essentially what is commonly called "height above sea level." It is a physically meaningful height: water flows from high to low H, so H is essential for engineering and hydraulic applications. Ellipsoidal height (h) is the height of a point measured perpendicularly above the surface of the reference ellipsoid. GPS receivers directly compute ellipsoidal height because they use the WGS84 ellipsoid as their reference. The relationship between the two is given by the Geoid separation (undulation), denoted N: N = h − H, or equivalently H = h − N. The Geoid separation N varies globally and can be positive or negative. In most practical applications, GPS-derived ellipsoidal heights must be converted to orthometric heights using a geoid model, because engineering systems require physically meaningful heights referenced to mean sea level.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "h − H = N" → GPS gives h (ellipsoidal), you need H (physical). N = the gap between math and reality. "h is higher, H is humble (geoid)."
Q19 What is WGS84, and why is it used as the standard ellipsoid for GPS? State its key parameters. ▼
Lecture 3🔥 Very Likely
MODEL ANSWER
WGS84 (World Geodetic System 1984) is the global reference ellipsoid adopted by the United States Department of Defense for the GPS satellite navigation system. It is a geocentric ellipsoid — its origin is at the Earth's center of mass — making it suitable for global positioning. Its key parameters are: semi-major axis a = 6,378,137 m (equatorial radius), semi-minor axis b = 6,356,752 m (polar radius), and flattening f = 1/298.257. WGS84 is used as the standard for GPS because it provides a globally consistent geometric framework, enabling precise positioning anywhere on Earth. It was established by the International Union of Geodesy and Geophysics (IUGG) and is the reference for all modern GNSS systems. When a GPS receiver reports latitude, longitude, and height, these values are referenced to WGS84.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "84 = 1984 + 6-3-1" → WGS84 from 1984; a=6,378,137 (6 million); b=6,356,752 (lower than a by ~21km); f=1/298 (flat like 1/300).
Q20 Distinguish between ITRS (International Terrestrial Reference System) and ITRF (International Terrestrial Reference Frame). ▼
Lecture 3
MODEL ANSWER
The ITRS (International Terrestrial Reference System) is the theoretical conceptual framework — a set of conventions, constants, and models that define a geocentric, Earth-fixed Cartesian coordinate system. It specifies that the origin is at Earth's center of mass, the Z-axis points toward the mean North Pole (as defined by the International Earth Rotation Service), and the X-axis points toward the prime meridian. The ITRF (International Terrestrial Reference Frame) is the practical physical realization of the ITRS — the actual set of coordinates assigned to a network of precisely surveyed ground stations distributed globally, determined primarily through GPS observations. The ITRF is periodically updated (e.g., ITRF2020) to account for tectonic plate motion, which causes station positions to change over time. In essence, ITRS defines the rules, and ITRF provides the actual numbers that implement those rules.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 System vs Frame: ITRS = the rulebook (concept). ITRF = the scoreboard (real numbers). Like a measurement standard (System) vs a certified ruler (Frame).
Q21 What is a map projection? Describe the three main types and explain what distortions each introduces. ▼
Lecture 3🔥 Likely
MODEL ANSWER
A map projection is the mathematical transformation of the curved surface of the Earth (sphere or ellipsoid) onto a flat two-dimensional plane. Since no projection can perfectly preserve all properties simultaneously, every projection involves trade-offs between shape, area, distance, and direction. The three main types are classified by the geometric surface used. Cylindrical projections wrap a cylinder around the globe; the Mercator projection is the most famous example — it preserves angles and shapes locally (conformal) but severely distorts area near the poles, making high-latitude regions appear much larger than they are. Conical projections place a cone on the globe, touching along one or two standard parallels; they are well-suited for mid-latitude regions and used in national mapping systems. Azimuthal (planar) projections project from a flat plane tangent to the globe, preserving directions from the center; they are ideal for polar regions or showing distances from a single point. The choice of projection depends on the geographic region and purpose of the map.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "Can Cats Avoid?" → Cylindrical (roll a paper), Conical (paper cone hat), Azimuthal (flat plate on globe). Mercator = cylindrical = great for navigation, bad for area near poles.
Q22 Describe the Mercator projection — what properties does it preserve and what are its major limitations? ▼
Lecture 3
MODEL ANSWER
The Mercator projection is a cylindrical conformal map projection developed by Gerardus Mercator in 1569. It is constructed by mathematically projecting the globe onto a cylinder that is tangent at the equator, then unrolling the cylinder. Its primary property is conformality — it preserves angles and local shapes, meaning rhumb lines (lines of constant compass bearing) appear as straight lines, which made it invaluable for maritime navigation. However, the Mercator projection introduces severe area distortion that increases with latitude. Regions near the poles are greatly exaggerated: Greenland appears roughly the same size as Africa on a Mercator map, even though Africa is approximately 14 times larger. The poles cannot be shown at all because the projection extends to infinity at 90°. Despite its limitations for portraying area, it remains widely used for web mapping (Google Maps uses a variant) because of its mathematical simplicity and navigational utility.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "Mercator = Sailor's friend, Geographer's enemy" → Perfect for navigation (straight compass lines), terrible for showing real sizes (Greenland = Africa? No!).
Q23 Describe the workflow for transforming spatial data from one map projection to another using a different datum. ▼
Lecture 3
MODEL ANSWER
Transforming data between two coordinate systems with different datums is a multi-step process that cannot be done in a single direct conversion. The workflow proceeds as follows. First, convert the projected coordinates in System A (e.g., Easting/Northing) back to geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) referenced to Datum A — this is an inverse projection. Second, apply a datum transformation to convert the geographic coordinates from Datum A to Datum B — this involves a mathematical transformation accounting for differences in ellipsoid shape, orientation, and origin between the two datums, commonly using methods such as the Helmert 7-parameter transformation. Third, apply the forward projection of System B to convert the geographic coordinates referenced to Datum B into the projected coordinates of System B (e.g., UTM Easting/Northing). This three-step process ensures that both geometric and physical reference frame differences are correctly accounted for, preventing positioning errors that could reach meters or even tens of meters if transformation steps are omitted.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "Unproject → Switch Datum → Re-project" → Always go through geographic (lat/lon) as the bridge. Can't jump between projections directly if datums differ.
Q24 What is a pseudorange in GPS positioning, and why is it called "pseudo"? ▼
Lecture 3🔥 Likely
MODEL ANSWER
A pseudorange is the apparent distance between a GPS satellite and a receiver, calculated from the travel time of the satellite's radio signal multiplied by the speed of light. It is called "pseudo" (meaning false or approximate) because it is not the true geometric distance between the satellite and receiver. The discrepancy arises primarily from the receiver clock bias — GPS receivers use inexpensive quartz clocks that are not perfectly synchronized with the highly precise atomic clocks aboard GPS satellites. This clock error, if uncorrected, translates to significant position errors (a 1-microsecond clock error corresponds to approximately 300 m of range error). Because the receiver clock error is common to all satellite measurements, it can be estimated and eliminated as a fourth unknown in the positioning equations, alongside the three position coordinates (X, Y, Z). This is why at least four satellites are needed for a complete 3D position fix — to solve four unknowns simultaneously.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "Pseudo = not real yet" → Pseudorange = GPS distance estimate before fixing the clock mistake. True range = pseudorange once clock bias (Δt) is removed.
Q25 Explain mathematically why at least four satellites are required to determine a 3D position using GPS. ▼
Lecture 3🔥 Very Likely
MODEL ANSWER
GPS positioning requires solving for four unknowns simultaneously: the receiver's position in three-dimensional space (X, Y, Z) and the receiver clock bias (Δt). Each satellite provides one pseudorange measurement, which yields one equation. In mathematics, solving for n unknowns requires at least n independent equations. Therefore, to solve for four unknowns (X, Y, Z, Δt), we need a minimum of four pseudorange measurements from four different satellites. With only three satellites, the system has three equations but four unknowns — the problem is underdetermined and cannot be solved for 3D position with clock correction. With four satellites, the system is exactly determined: the four equations can be solved simultaneously to obtain the three position coordinates and eliminate the receiver clock error. Using five or more satellites provides redundancy, enabling least-squares adjustment for improved accuracy and integrity monitoring to detect erroneous measurements.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "3 for space, 1 for time" → Three satellites locate you in space (X,Y,Z). The 4th satellite fixes your clock (Δt). 4 unknowns = 4 satellites. Simple algebra!
Q26 Distinguish between spatial (geographic) and planar coordinate systems, and explain when each is used. ▼
Lecture 3
MODEL ANSWER
Spatial or geographic coordinate systems describe positions on the curved surface of the Earth using angular measurements. Geographic coordinates (latitude φ and longitude λ) express position relative to the equator and prime meridian, both measured in degrees. Three-dimensional Cartesian geocentric coordinates (X, Y, Z) express position relative to Earth's center of mass, used in ITRS. These systems are ideal for global applications and long-distance navigation because they inherently account for Earth's curvature. Planar coordinate systems, by contrast, project the curved Earth surface onto a flat two-dimensional plane, expressing positions as Easting and Northing distances (typically in metres). Common examples include Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) and national grid systems. Planar systems are preferred for local and regional GIS applications because flat-surface geometry simplifies distance and area calculations. However, they introduce distortions that grow with distance from the projection's central point, making them unsuitable for global-scale analysis.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "Sphere vs Sheet" → Geographic = on the sphere (degrees, global). Planar = on a flat sheet (metres, local). Use geographic for GPS navigation; use planar for GIS measurement and mapping.
Q27 How were local horizontal datums established before GPS, and what were their limitations? ▼
Lecture 3
MODEL ANSWER
Before GPS, local horizontal datums were established through classical geodetic surveying techniques, primarily triangulation and trilateration. Triangulation involved measuring the angles of a network of triangles across the landscape from precisely known base points, allowing positions to propagate outward with high accuracy using trigonometry. Trilateration measured distances between points using precise instruments such as electronic distance measurement (EDM) devices, rather than angles. These techniques produced networks of control points tied to a locally optimal reference ellipsoid — chosen and positioned to best fit the shape of the geoid in that particular region. The limitations of local datums are significant: each country or region used a different ellipsoid and a different datum origin, meaning coordinates were incompatible across borders. Local datums are not geocentric, so they cannot be used for global positioning or satellite navigation without transformation. The adoption of WGS84 as a global standard largely overcame these limitations, though datum transformations remain necessary when working with legacy datasets.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "Before GPS: Measure angles, chain the country" → Triangulation = angle chains. Local ellipsoid = fitted to your country only. Problem: Europe uses different datum from Asia — incompatible!
Q28 Describe how Earth Observation (EO) data supports rapid damage assessment during disaster response, citing specific data types. ▼
Lecture 1
MODEL ANSWER
Earth Observation data plays an essential role in rapid damage assessment during disaster response by providing timely, spatially comprehensive information that ground-based teams cannot gather quickly. SAR (Synthetic Aperture Radar) data, such as from Sentinel-1, is particularly valuable because it operates regardless of cloud cover, smoke, or time of day — conditions common during and after disasters. By comparing pre- and post-event SAR images, analysts detect changes in backscatter that indicate building collapse, flooding, or landslides. Optical imagery, such as from Sentinel-2 or very-high-resolution commercial satellites like Pleiades, provides visually interpretable imagery for mapping flood extent, debris fields, and infrastructure damage. LiDAR data enables precise three-dimensional assessment of terrain changes and structural damage. UAV/drone imagery offers ultra-high resolution imagery of specific damaged areas at low cost. Together, these data streams enable the production of rapid damage maps within hours of a disaster, guiding the deployment of emergency responders to the most affected areas.

🧠 "SOUL" → SAR (cloud-proof), Optical (visual), UAV (high-res local), LiDAR (3D terrain). Each sensor fills a gap the others leave.
Q29 What does "Build Back Better" mean in the context of disaster recovery, and how does it relate to SFDRR? ▼
Lecture 1
MODEL ANSWER
"Build Back Better" (BBB) is a principle in disaster recovery that advocates for restoring communities and infrastructure in a way that reduces future risk, rather than simply reconstructing what existed before. The core idea is that a post-disaster reconstruction period presents a unique opportunity — often called a "window of opportunity" — to incorporate improved building standards, hazard-resistant design, better land-use planning, and stronger governance that reduces future vulnerability. For example, after a flood, reconstructing buildings with flood-resistant materials, elevating floor levels, or relocating at-risk settlements to safer areas. BBB was formally incorporated into Priority 4 of the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (SFDRR, 2015–2030), making it a cornerstone of international DRR policy. It represents the shift from merely responding to disasters to using recovery as an entry point for long-term resilience building — ensuring that societies emerge from disasters stronger and better prepared than before.

Q30 Explain the concept of ellipsoid flattening. What does a larger or smaller flattening value indicate about Earth's shape? ▼
Lecture 3
MODEL ANSWER
The flattening of a reference ellipsoid is a dimensionless parameter that quantifies how much the ellipsoid deviates from a perfect sphere. It is defined as f = (a − b) / a, where a is the semi-major (equatorial) axis and b is the semi-minor (polar) axis. For the WGS84 ellipsoid used by GPS, f = 1/298.257, which means the polar radius is shorter than the equatorial radius by about one part in 298, or approximately 21 kilometres. A flattening value of 0 would indicate a perfect sphere (a = b). A larger flattening value indicates a more oblate (squashed) shape, while a smaller value indicates a shape closer to a sphere. Earth's flattening arises from its rotation — centrifugal force causes material to bulge at the equator and flatten at the poles. The small but non-zero flattening of WGS84 means that precise positioning, particularly in the north-south direction, must account for this elliptical geometry rather than assuming a spherical Earth.
MEMORY TRICK
🧠 "f = (a−b)/a — bigger f = more squashed" → f=0: perfect ball. f=1: completely flat disk. Earth's f≈1/298: barely squashed, like a ball someone sat on very lightly. Polar radius is ~21km shorter than equatorial.
